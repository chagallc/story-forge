Kesh counted the stairs going down into the eighth forge the way he counted everything: under his breath, automatic, a habit older than his apprenticeship. Forty-one steps. Two hundred and six strikes on the wheel before the first bell. Four days until the journeyman evaluation.

The coal chute had been filled overnight. He noticed it because the yard always looked poorer on delivery mornings, the pile lower, the sky lighter where the mountain had eaten more of it. Four baskets this week. Last quarter, two.

He put his name on the day's sheet, took the first grinding slot, and let the wheel decide the shape of his morning. Six years had taught his hands to read steel through the leather. Six years of scars, some of them still new enough to itch. The blade under his fingers was honest the way metal was always honest: it would hold an edge or it wouldn't, and no ledger anywhere could change that.

"Seventy-one," he said, "seventy-two."

"You'll ruin the bevel talking." Oren Vael stood in the doorway of the fitting room with the day's invoices clipped to a slate. Fifty-seven years old and he still stood like a man braced against weather, feet wide, weight forward. He had given Kesh the first slot every morning for three years running, even when paying work came in slow. Everyone in Vhalun's lower terraces said Master Vael was the generous one. "Seventy strikes to the shoulder pass, always. You know the count better than I do."

"Coal came early," Kesh said. He kept his eyes on the wheel. "Fourth basket this week. That's double the quarter's draw."

"So it is."

"The output sheet doesn't show double. I checked Sunday. Twenty-two finished blades, eleven repairs. Same as autumn."

Oren looked at the coal chute, then at Kesh, and quoted a price. "Vhalun coal's up nine coppers the load since the canal froze. Traders on the east stairs are holding stock. A man who buys before the peak spends more and calls it foresight." He clipped the slate to his belt. "Your evaluation is Thursday. The wheel is forty paces to your left."

Kesh went back to the wheel. He did seventy strikes to the shoulder pass, then seventy more on the second blade, and thought about nine coppers the load. A man with foresight bought coal. A man with a reason bought twice as much and said nothing about it.

The debt collector had come to the house twice this month. His mother had paid the second visit with the mending money and told the man, through the door, that her son was nearly a journeyman and journeymen earned a full wage. The man had said winter comes Thursday after next, by his count. The house was the collateral. It had been the collateral since the year the guild paid out nothing for his father, because the contract had been bought and sold twice before the collapse, and the men who held the paper at the end held no duty to anyone under it. Kesh had been thirteen. He remembered the price of everything from that year and almost nothing else.

Midmorning, the dock dray came. That was normal; the eighth forge shipped to the eastern dock every ten days, plow fittings mostly, cheap work nobody in the city wanted. Kesh helped load because load-bearing work counted on the evaluation sheet, and the sheet was the whole week.

The crates were wrong.

They came down from the fitting room already packed, which was itself unusual. Oren packed his own shipments and stenciled them himself, VIII-F in the guild template, every time. These had been stenciled before Kesh ever saw them. He lifted the first one onto the dray bed and felt the weight register before his arms did. Forty pounds heavier than plow fittings. He set it down and read the seal.

IX-M.

The ninth forge's mark, the M-suffix for military contract work, stamped in black over crate after crate. Six crates. The eighth forge shipped none of the ninth's orders. The ninth forge had run two apprentices and a dying fire all winter; the whole terrace knew it. The ninth forge couldn't fill a military contract for knives, let alone whatever these weighed.

"Heavy for plow fittings," Kesh said.

"They're not plow fittings." Oren came out with the last crate himself, carried it one-handed against his hip, and slid it onto the dray without a sound. "Mix-up at the ninth. Their cart wouldn't make the dock pass in this weather, so ours goes down instead. Simple freight. Freight's paid by the load either way." He patted the crate's lid the way he patted finished work. "Twelve coppers a load, east run. Good money for a morning."

"Whose contract is it?"

Oren counted something on his slate. Then he said, "Your hands are bleeding through the left glove. Change the wrap before you grind again, or the evaluation board will smell it on you." He turned back inside.

The dray pulled out at noon with six crates and no manifest Kesh could see. Oren had walked the driver to the stairhead and spoken to him there, low, and come back alone.

Kesh spent the afternoon finishing a repair lot of eleven agricultural blades, and he counted, because counting was what his head did when it had nowhere to put itself. Eleven blades at market rate was thirty-one coppers. Eleven blades' worth of coal, at Oren's own forge-standard draw, was nine coppers. Two hundred and six strikes a blade, forty-one steps, twelve coppers a load. The coal account had doubled. The shipping volume had not. The eighth forge was buying heat it never sold and moving crates that weighed like rifles and wore another forge's name.

He wanted to ask. He had one question ready and it was a good one, built all afternoon: the ninth forge's order book, public record at the guild hall, would show what contract the IX-M crates fulfilled, and if it showed none, then Oren had just put his forge's mark on military freight for somebody with no paper at all. That was hanging work. That was the kind of thing the guild war ate people over.

And Oren Vael wrote the evaluation Thursday. Kesh needed the journeyman stamp, the full wage, eleven months of wages to clear his mother's debt before the collector's winter count. One question in the wrong tone and the slot could go to Tavin Rul, who asked nothing of anyone, ever. In the third-rate guilds, loyalty was the whole purse. Everyone knew what happened to apprentices who counted another man's coal.

He banked the fire at dusk, hung his gloves, and stood a moment at the fitting-room doorway where the day's invoices were still clipped to the slate. Twenty-two blades sold. Thirty-one coppers. Nine coppers of draw, times two baskets that weren't his forge's work. He could almost balance it in his head if one number would stay still, and it wouldn't, and that was the shape of the thing he couldn't yet see.

Oren was at the far bench, back turned, chalk in hand, writing something behind the quench barrel where the light didn't reach.

Kesh looked at the guild hall door, the one he could reach before it closed tonight, and at the master's back, and counted under his breath, one, two, three, and did not leave.
