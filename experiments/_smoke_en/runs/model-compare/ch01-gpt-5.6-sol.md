Kesh counted forty-two turns of the grinding wheel before the blade stopped fighting him.

“Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two.”

He lifted the short sword from the rest and sighted along its edge. Gray morning lay in the high windows. Against it, the steel showed every fault.

One flat place near the point.

He set the blade back.

Master Oren looked up from the worktable. “You’ll grind it hollow.”

“It’s flat.”

“It’s half a hair.”

“Still flat.”

“Then you have nine minutes.”

Kesh leaned into the treadle. The wheel spun under his boot. Grit struck his apron and bare forearms. He held the sword steady while the scars across his fingers pulled tight with the strain.

Nine minutes until the guild examiner arrived. Nine minutes before Oren laid three pieces on the table and declared whether six years at the eighth forge had made Kesh fit to work for wages.

Journeyman wages.

Kesh had worked the sum before dawn. Thirty-two silver in his mother’s debt box. Five more due at month’s end. If Oren advanced him three months against a journeyman contract, the collector could be paid before the first hard frost.

The red notice in their front window said otherwise. PAYMENT OR SEIZURE. The collector had pressed it flat with his thumb and told Kesh’s mother that a roof had value even in winter.

The edge brightened. Kesh counted six more turns, then raised the blade.

Oren crossed the floor and tested it with a square thumbnail. His left hand had two missing fingertips. He could still find half a hair of bad work in the dark.

“Better,” he said.

“Good enough?”

“Good enough costs twelve silver. Better costs eighteen.”

Kesh waited.

Oren placed the sword beside a socketed spearhead and a woodcutter’s axe on the evaluation table. “Don’t ask a question when you can read the price.”

Hooves clattered in the alley.

Kesh looked toward the open loading doors. “Examiner?”

“At this hour?” Oren checked the brass clock above the furnace. “No.”

A wagon backed through the doors, guided by a boy in an eastern-dock coat. Its rear wheels bumped over the threshold. Two crates filled the bed, each long enough for polearms and deep enough to need four men.

The driver slapped the wagon rail. “Vael?”

Oren wiped his hands. “Here.”

“Transfer from Ninth.”

The eighth forge had its seal cut into every finished crate: an eight-spoked wheel inside a square. These crates bore black stencil instead.

IX-M.

Kesh read it twice.

Oren gave the mark one glance. “Put them by the east wall.”

The driver jumped down. “Dock order says they go straight through.”

“Dock orders cost dock rates. Floor storage is three coppers per hour.”

The man swore and waved in the porters.

Kesh watched them lift the first crate. Fresh iron bands circled it. No soot. No rain marks. When one porter lost his grip, the crate struck the wagon bed with a dense, close-packed knock.

“Hands,” Oren said.

Kesh turned back.

The sword lay waiting. Beside it, Oren had placed a cloth purse. Its mouth hung open enough to show silver.

“For coal,” Oren said. “Hessa wants payment before noon. Take the lower street. It saves twenty-eight steps.”

Kesh looked at the clock. Seven minutes.

“You said the examiner was coming.”

“He is.”

“The coal yard is fifteen minutes there and back.”

“Twelve if you run.”

The porters brought the second crate in. Oren did not watch them. He adjusted the spearhead until it sat square with the edge of the table.

Kesh took the purse. It weighed too much for a routine order.

At the ledger stand by the door, Oren’s coal book lay open. Kesh had copied its columns every sixth day since midsummer. The latest total had been inked in Oren’s narrow hand.

One hundred sixty-four crowns.

Kesh set the purse on the page.

“Master.”

“Six minutes.”

“This quarter’s coal is double.”

Oren’s hand stopped over the axe.

Kesh ran a blackened finger beneath the figure. “We used eighty-one last quarter. Seventy-nine before that.”

“Coal was nine bits a sack in spring. It is fourteen now.”

“That doesn’t make double.”

“A cart costs two silver from the north yard. Three from Hessa. Wet sacks lose one part in twelve by weight.”

“We haven’t made double.”

Oren turned the axe head toward the window. “A finished sword brings eighteen silver retail. Twelve guild. Eight if sold in lots above fifty.”

Kesh looked at him.

There were no lots above fifty. In three months they had finished nineteen swords, thirty spearheads, and eleven axes. Kesh knew because he had ground every edge.

The wagon driver coughed near the east wall. One crate sat beside him. The other had already gone.

Kesh looked toward the rear passage. Its door was swinging shut.

Oren picked up the coal purse and pressed it into Kesh’s hand. “Five minutes. Bring me a receipt.”

“You didn’t answer.”

“Journeymen are paid for answers. Apprentices are paid in instruction.”

“I’m not paid.”

“Exactly.”

The driver laughed once, then covered it with another cough.

Heat climbed Kesh’s neck. He closed his scarred fingers around the purse.

Oren stepped closer. Soot had settled in the lines around his mouth. “Your work is sound. Your recommendation can be signed today.”

Kesh’s grip loosened.

“Today?”

“At noon. Examiner or no examiner.”

Guild recommendations did not work that way. The examiner tested. The master witnessed. Both seals went on the contract.

Oren glanced at the open coal book. “There are delays in the guild. Men lose places while clerks measure rules. I can hold a place at the eighth for four crowns.”

“Four?”

“Deducted over twelve weeks.”

“And an advance?”

Oren’s gaze moved to Kesh’s patched cuff. “How much?”

“Thirty-seven.”

The clock ticked once. Twice.

Oren took a key from his belt and opened the iron cashbox under the worktable. He counted coins into stacks of ten, each click clean and separate.

Kesh had imagined this moment every night for a month. He would ask. Oren would complain about margins. Kesh would offer winter hours. The bargain would hurt, but the red notice would come down.

Oren counted out thirty-seven silver.

The amount sat between them.

“A year’s contract,” Oren said. “No outside commissions. No guild petitions without my approval. You keep my books as you’ve kept them.”

Kesh stared at the coins. “Which books?”

Oren closed the cashbox.

The examiner’s cane struck the alley stones outside.

Oren swept the silver back into one stack. “Decide before he enters.”

Kesh heard his mother scraping frost from the inside of their window last winter. He saw the red notice under the collector’s thumb.

Then he saw IX-M in wet black paint.

He took the coal purse.

Oren’s shoulders lowered by the width of a finger.

“I’ll get the receipt,” Kesh said.

The examiner appeared in the doorway as Kesh slipped past him. White guild braid. Silver-headed cane. A face pinched by the climb.

“Apprentice Duran?” the man asked.

Kesh kept walking.

He ran to Hessa’s yard in eleven minutes and returned in twelve. He paid fourteen bits per sack, demanded the weight, and counted the wagon charge twice. While Hessa’s clerk wrote the receipt, Kesh turned it over and listed the eighth forge’s output from memory.

Nineteen swords.

Thirty spearheads.

Eleven axes.

At the forge door, the examiner’s voice carried over the hammering.

“Your master says you withdrew.”

Kesh entered. Oren stood beside the evaluation table. The sword, spearhead, and axe had been removed.

The silver was gone too.

“I had an errand,” Kesh said.

The examiner tapped his cane against the floor. “A journeyman who leaves his test leaves his place.”

Oren held out his hand for the receipt.

Kesh gave it to him blank side up.

Oren read the figures on the back. His thumb covered the final line.

“You count well,” he said.

“I grind better.”

“So you do.”

The examiner looked between them. “Shall I mark him absent?”

Oren folded the receipt once. “Come tomorrow.”

The cane struck the floor. “My return fee is six silver.”

“I know the price.”

When the examiner had gone, Oren fed the folded receipt into the furnace. Kesh watched the paper curl black.

“East wall,” Oren said. “Those crates need moving before dusk.”

Only one crate remained there.

Kesh took up the iron hooks. He worked until Oren left for the guild hall, then until the eastern-dock driver rattled away with an empty wagon.

At closing, Kesh barred the front doors and counted the day’s finished pieces.

Two knives. One mattock head. The short sword.

He wrote them on the inside of his wrist with chalk. Beneath them, he wrote the coal delivered and the coal already charged.

The totals refused to meet.

Across the dark forge, Oren’s private ledger stand waited beside the quench barrel. The upper book recorded wages and sales. Below it sat a narrow drawer Kesh had dusted for six years and never opened.

He crossed the floor, counting his steps under his breath.

“Eight. Nine. Ten.”

At eleven, he set his burned hand on the drawer pull.
