Kesh counted ninety-seven turns of the grinding wheel before the bell above the eighth forge rang for dawn.

At one hundred, he changed his grip.

The blade blank hissed against the stone. Sparks struck his apron and died in the old burn holes. His left thumb had split open along a scar from last winter; he kept it wrapped in oiled rag and pressed harder with the heel of his hand.

“Angle,” Master Vael said.

Kesh adjusted the blade by a hair.

“Again.”

The wheel groaned. Water ran brown beneath it, carrying steel dust toward the drain. Across the forge floor, four men worked the morning heat. Hammers rose and fell. Bellows coughed. The furnace mouth showed a hard orange square under the soot-black hood.

Kesh counted thirty strokes on each side of the edge. Thirty-one. Thirty-two.

He could do this.

A journeyman evaluation meant his own mark on small contracts. It meant full wages instead of apprentice scraps. It meant he could go to Lenna Voss at the debt office with silver in both hands and tell her to strike Duran from her winter book.

His mother’s house had a cracked roof and one good room left downstairs. Lenna had stood beneath that roof three days ago, dry beneath a waxed hood, and set a red notice on the table.

Before spring, she had said.

Kesh lifted the blade from the wheel.

Master Vael took it without looking up from the ledger balanced on a barrel. He ran one thumb along the edge, then held it against the forge light. His beard had more white in it than black now. He wore a clean leather apron despite the ash settling everywhere else.

“Too much pressure at the heel,” he said.

“It’ll hold.”

“Hold what?”

Kesh watched the master turn the blade. “A man’s hand.”

“A butcher’s knife holds a man’s hand. A sword holds when another sword bites it.” Vael set the blank down. “You want an evaluation, Duran, stop making arguments for work nobody ordered.”

“Yes, Master.”

Vael tapped the ledger with one blunt finger. “Six more blades. Two hinge sets. Then you may ask me whether you are ready.”

Kesh swallowed the answer that rose in his throat. Six blades meant three days if the forge gave him steady work. Three days brought him closer to the end of the month, when Lenna Voss came for payment.

He reached for the next blank.

The yard gate banged open.

Two wagon horses dragged a load through the slush outside, iron shoes slipping on the cobbles. Everyone near the furnace glanced toward the door. Deliveries meant work. Work meant coin, if the guild clerk remembered who had done it.

A porter shoved inside backward, shoulders against a crate nearly as tall as he was. Another followed with a pry bar. Cold daylight came in with them, gray and thin.

“Eastern dock,” the first porter called. “Where do you want these?”

Vael closed his ledger.

“Against the north wall,” he said. “Keep them dry.”

The porters wrestled the first crate across the floor. Kesh saw the stenciled mark as it passed the grinding wheel: a black circle broken by three short bars, with letters beneath.

IX-M.

It was not the eighth forge’s stamp. Kesh knew their mark. Eight small teeth around an anvil, crooked because the old die had cracked before he was born.

The second crate bore the same mark.

“Master,” Kesh said.

Vael did not look at him. “Your wheel is turning cold.”

“That seal.”

“Cargo seals belong to cargo.”

“It isn’t ours.”

“That is why it was painted on someone else’s wood.”

The porter snorted, then remembered himself and pushed harder. Vael’s eyes came up. The man lowered his head.

Kesh set the unfinished blade down. A question could cost him three days. It could cost him the evaluation entirely. The guild had plenty of boys who could stand at a wheel until their shoulders locked.

But the crate smelled wrong.

New pine, lamp oil, and the sharp mineral scent of packed steel.

“Are we making dock fittings now?” he asked.

Vael looked at him for a long moment. The furnace clicked behind them as a coal seam broke.

“Do you intend to buy dock fittings?”

“No.”

“Then put your hands where they earn you wages.”

The words were mild. Vael used that tone in front of customers, when he offered water to men whose purses he was emptying.

Kesh picked up the blade.

The porters carried four crates to the north wall. Vael signed their slate with a quick scratch, paid them from the locked cash box, and sent them back into the snow. The gate shut. The forge swallowed the sound of wheels.

Kesh put the blade to the stone again.

One. Two. Three.

At forty, the coal clerk arrived.

Pella wore a wool cap pulled down over her ears and carried a narrow account book under one arm. She never entered the hot part of the forge if she could help it. Heat turned her cheeks red and made her spectacles slide down her nose.

“Master Vael,” she called.

Vael was checking a rivet batch at the bench. “Leave it.”

“Need your thumbprint.”

“For coal?”

“For the quarter.”

He wiped his hand on his apron and crossed to her. Kesh kept grinding, though the blade had reached its proper line several strokes ago.

Pella opened the book.

“Eighth Forge,” she read, “delivery totals confirmed through Frostday. Three hundred and sixty sacks.”

Vael pressed his thumb into the ink pad.

Kesh’s wheel slowed.

Three hundred and sixty.

He had helped unload coal twice a week since the first frost. Twelve sacks here. Ten there. Sometimes fifteen. He counted because numbers filled the spaces between hammer blows. The eighth forge had taken one hundred and eighty-two sacks by his count.

Maybe he had missed deliveries. Maybe men had worked while he slept in the loft with his hands throbbing under the blanket.

Still.

“Three hundred and sixty?” he said.

Pella glanced at him.

Vael’s thumb hovered above the page.

“We used half that last quarter,” Kesh said. “Output hasn’t doubled.”

The other apprentices found reasons to look at their work. Even the bellows man kept his boot still for a beat.

Vael set his thumbprint on the paper.

“A sack costs eleven coppers at summer rate,” he said. “Fourteen after the first freeze. You want to know why a forge buys coal, price the cold.”

Kesh stared at him.

Pella shut the account book. “I’ll have the receipt copied.”

“Do that,” Vael said.

She left quickly, her boots tapping across the stones.

Kesh returned to the wheel. The stone spun beneath his fingers. He could feel Vael at the bench without turning around.

Eleven coppers. Fourteen. A price was an answer only if a person had asked about price.

By noon, Kesh had ruined one blade.

The edge went blue at the tip where his attention broke. He saw it at once. There was no hiding heat stain from a master smith.

Vael took the blank from him, held it up, and said nothing.

“I’ll pay for the steel,” Kesh said.

“With what?”

Kesh looked toward the wage slate nailed beside the door. His name stood under three others. Apprentice rate. Six coppers a day, less for meals.

Vael dropped the spoiled blade into the scrap bin. “You pay with tomorrow’s work.”

“Yes, Master.”

The words scraped on the way out.

At dusk, the furnace was banked. The other men filed into the yard, shoulders bent against the snow. Kesh stayed to sweep steel scale into the ash buckets. Vael sat in the office alcove, ledger open, lamp burning at his elbow.

Kesh swept past the north wall.

Four crates waited there under canvas.

IX-M. IX-M. IX-M. IX-M.

He counted the finished goods stacked for the week: twelve hinge sets, eighteen knife blanks, six shovel heads, thirty-seven rivet bundles, four sword billets awaiting temper. He counted the scrap bin. He counted the charcoal barrels, the oil jars, the water buckets changed since morning.

Then he took the chalk nub from beside the wage slate.

On the back of a broken packing board, where the wall hid it from the office, he wrote the first number.

Coal: 360.

Vael turned a page behind him.

Kesh lifted the chalk again, holding it one finger’s width from the wood.
