The shoulder didn't open the door. It didn't need to. The latch was brass, old, and Kesh had carried water past this door six years, and six years of watching a man's hands do a thing teaches a boy how the thing is done. The lock gave on the fourth try. Two coins' worth of skill, he thought, and filed the thought away like a coin itself.

The drawer held the guild ledger. He didn't touch it. He searched the rest of the room fast, lamp down low, and found nothing, no second book, no locked strongbox, no loose papers at all. A tidy room. A room scrubbed by a man who counted everything, even the things he didn't write down.

Kesh stood in the middle of it with lamp-black drying on his palm, and understood that if there was a second ledger it didn't live here. It lived wherever the coal was burning.

So he made a plan, because that was what his father had done when the mountain came down: not run to the guild, not shout, count. He would take the unofficial night shift. Tam Breel had said that morning, casual, half out the door, that the shop needed a man on nights now, that the work was simple, that it paid in coin Kesh could carry home same as any other. The pay was double the day rate. The pay was the point, Tam had said, and Kesh had said yes before the master even knew, because double pay was half of Culvane's number in six weeks, and because whoever fed the night fire fed the truth into it too.

He would work the shift. He would keep his head down and his mouth shut and he would find out what the fire was making and who came to carry it away. Then he would have a count, dates, weights, names, a count good enough to lay in front of the guild assessor, stone on stone, and the stone would not be his word against a master smith's. It would be arithmetic.

Arithmetic had bought his father's life for nine coppers. Arithmetic could sell the eighth forge.

The first night shift, Tuesday, he was given a coal sack, a billet, and a pattern, and told to grind. The billet was long and narrow with a reinforced spine. A barrel furniture blank, he thought at first, and then he turned it under the lamp and knew better. He had seen rifles on the caravans. He knew what the spine channel was for.

He ground it. The wheel screamed. Seventeen strikes per pass, sixteen if his wrist was good. His wrist was not good. He counted every strike and hated every count, because his hands knew this steel, knew it the way you know a voice on the other side of a door. The temper color, the way it rang on the quench. He had been finishing billets like this one for weeks. The eleven sabers in the rack beside him shared a heat with it. His own grind wheel had fed it.

Tuesday night, Wednesday night, Thursday. He worked, and he watched, and he copied everything he could into his head the way he copied tallies into his palm. The man who came for the crates was called Sellen; he paid in Merrin-minted silver, unclipped, and he called Oren "Master Vael" with a bow that no third-rate guild forge had earned in a decade. The tallies behind the quench barrel grew. Kesh redrew them himself now, that was the job, that was the joke of it, his own hand keeping the count of his own betrayal. Nine strokes, nine strokes, four. Rifles. Barrels and furniture, enough for a company.

And on Thursday, near the hour before dawn, with Sellen's cart already loaded and the banked fires ticking, Oren Vael came down the forty-one stairs.

Kesh went still by the quench barrel, chalk in his hand.

The old man crossed the workshop floor without a lamp, which meant he didn't need one, which meant he'd walked it this many nights the color of the walk didn't matter. He stopped at the rack of finished work, lifted a barrel blank, sighted down it the way a man sights down a blade, and put it back. Then he came around the barrel and stood in front of Kesh, close enough that Kesh could smell the coal smoke that lived in his apron.

"Your wrist is better on nights," Oren said.

"Yes, Master."

"Quiet shop, nights. A man hears himself think." He took the chalk out of Kesh's slack fingers, turned it over once, and set it on the barrel rim. He drew no line. He looked at the wall behind the barrel, at the tallies, for a long moment, reading his own hand. And it was his hand. Kesh saw it now, at this distance, under the low lamp: the tallies were Oren's writing, heavy and exact, and the M in Tam's cramped script had been added later, a price note, a customer column. The hand that had taught Kesh to count strikes had counted rifles.

"Twenty-two hundred rounds of furniture a month," Oren said, in the fish-market voice. "At Merrin's rate, that is a number. At the guild's rate, that is nothing. You understand the difference between rates."

Kesh's mouth was dry. "Master, the mark on the crates. IX-M. The ninth forge went cold."

"Steel is warm somewhere." Oren picked up the chalk and dropped it in Kesh's palm, closing Kesh's scarred fingers around it. "Grind the next billet. Dawn shift is yours too; Tam will pay you for both." And he climbed the stairs, counting.

Kesh stood by the barrel with the chalk in his fist and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since Tuesday.

IX-M. Nine coppers. He had seen that mark before, long before it was stenciled on oak. He had seen it in the paper his mother kept folded in the bottom of her chest. The assignment of his father's contract, the compensation claim, the seal of the purchasing house at the bottom where the guild's seal should have been. House Merrin. The nine coppers. The crate seals were the same mark, dressed in guild format, wearing a cold forge's clothes. His master was not running freight. His master was arming the family that had paid nine coppers for his father and sold the debt at profit, and the steel Kesh had ground for six weeks, the sabers, the furniture, the best of the nine, the evaluation piece, was on its way to the border, to the passes where the war ate men the way his father's worksite had.

His rule was a short one and he had never broken it: you don't lie about metal. Tell a man a blade will hold an edge and it won't, that is a lie about metal. Grind the steel and stay quiet and let it travel, that was the same lie, longer.

But speaking cost Culvane's whole number. The guild council was half Merrin's; the assessor drank with Tam Breel's cousins. A word to the wrong desk and Oren never submitted a blade, and his mother stood in the road in spring holding a folded paper and nothing else. Yet the right desk. There was one man in Vhalun the Merrins did not drink with, one man they had spent two years trying to bury in lawsuits: Assessor General Corvan Hale, the guild's honest rogue, who had stripped two master smiths of their marks and survived it. Proof in Hale's hands was a knife. Proof in anyone else's was a confession Kesh would hang from.

Behind the quench barrel the tallies waited, fresh in his own hand now, twenty-two strokes and four. He had a count of rifles and no count of money, and Hale would want both. The second ledger, where Oren wrote what he truly sold, lived somewhere Kesh hadn't looked, and Oren was awake upstairs with a lamp, and dawn was two hours off, and Tam would arrive expecting two shifts paid for, coin in Kesh's palm, coin that bought silence as easy as it bought bread.

He could grind the billet and take the coin and be a liar with a roof over his mother. He could walk to Hale's office at first light with half a proof and lose everything, maybe, on a man's reputation instead of his own.

Forty-one stairs. He stood at the bottom of them and did not count.
